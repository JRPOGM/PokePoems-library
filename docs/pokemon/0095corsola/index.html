Polyp Neighborhood

No place is safe on this planet, is there?
No matter the length time has stretched
Counting the days lived where you do
Because the numbers are only going down

An invasive killer, the future
Present too soon for us to simply move past
Feet in the sand, coral cracking under their boot
How many waters can we pollute
Inland and beyond
Forgetting how it has helped us survive
How many homes do we run down
Families run out
Claiming owned land
So iron and smoke and poison can settle

How much will be left, of it and of us
When the blue water skies remain midnight oil black?

Corsola, the Coral Pokémon

“Corsola are a warmwater species. They congregate en masse in oceans of tropical or humid climates, like the southeastern waters of Hoenn and Melemele Island or the northeastern shores off Unova’s coast, though few more can be found in other regions across the world. Smaller pods of them exist elsewhere around the world as such because of years of hunting, both from people and from other Pokémon species. A Corsola’s branches are a sought-after treasure, those on their back second to the one that crowns each individual’s head, all filled with nutrients they survive on and other species can feed off; they escape capture from all hunters by breaking off their minor branches as a distraction. Johto is the only region to have locally enforced protection of the species as its population dwindled to endangered levels across it and the neighboring Kanto region.”
“One massive blow to the Corsola population resulted in the birth of a new branch of species found natively in the region of Galar. The industrial age of the region led to ecological reformation across the land, changing and endangering many different species of Pokémon forced to adapt to the sudden and poisonous damage done to nature. The quick spread of pollution and waste into the water endangered no species more than Corsola, whose numbers dropped drastically in only a short amount of time. Unable to survive naturally in contaminated waters, the species’ biology degraded and put their surviving members on the brink of death, and generations of pods born into these conditions over a few years eventually changed the species forever into a Ghost-type variant of Corsola that were no more than husks of their original shells.”
